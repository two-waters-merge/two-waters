The why

Design education should be
rooted in place.

Every design teacher in NSW is now required to teach from the DIT 7–10 Syllabus (2025). That syllabus places Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledge, technologies and design practices at the centre — not as a footnote. Two Rivers Merge exists to help teachers do that well.

01

The syllabus requires it

The NSW DIT 7–10 Syllabus (2025) names Indigenous design, materials and technologies as core curriculum — not optional enrichment. Every design project students undertake should investigate and apply First Nations design thinking.

02

First Nations voices define good design

This resource is not about First Nations culture — it is built with First Nations people. Gadigal knowledge holders and designers co-author the marking criteria. They define what respectful design looks like. Not us.

03

Materials should regenerate Country

Students choose materials that are regenerative, locally sourced, and carbon-conscious. We have mapped 20+ NSW regenerative material suppliers, 25+ First Nations-owned businesses, and 9 environmental restoration organisations by LGA.

04

Local design solves real problems

Each design brief is anchored to a real environmental challenge in a real LGA. Students design for the health of Sydney Harbour, a local river, a foreshore habitat. Their work connects to organisations actively restoring Country.

Every resource, design brief, material recommendation, and marking rubric in this project navigates back to the DIT syllabus. Three focus areas. Nine outcomes. Real-world project work. At least 4 design projects per student. At least one collaborative activity.

The how

How Two Rivers Merge works
in a Year 9 classroom.

Five scenarios. One Country. Real restoration organisations. Real First Nations businesses. Real marking — by Gadigal knowledge holders.

1

Choose your scenario

Teachers select one of five scenarios anchored to Sydney Harbour — Saltwater, Fresh Water, City Edge, Sky Country, or Land & Water. Each is a real environmental design challenge on Gadigal Country.

2

Walk on Country first

Students visit the harbour foreshore before designing. Observe, listen, document. Observation before action. Country before concept. This is the foundation of the folio.

3

Investigate with the neural map

Students use the interactive map to find local regenerative materials, First Nations designers whose practice connects to their scenario, and restoration organisations in their LGA.

4

Design, prototype, document

Students work through Investigation → Idea Generation → Production → Evaluation. Every stage documented in a Design and Production Folio. Material choices justified against regenerative and cultural criteria.

5

Marked by Gadigal voices

Cultural criteria are assessed by Gadigal knowledge holders who co-designed the rubric. Their mark carries equal weight to the teacher's technical assessment. Feedback is attributed: "This comes from Gadigal Country."

6

Reflect and improve

After each cycle, teachers and Gadigal markers reflect together. What gave you hope? What concerned you? What would you change? This reflection is as important as the project itself.

Before you begin

Cultural protocols
are not a checklist.

This project is built on relationships, not transactions. Before contacting any First Nations business, designer or knowledge holder:

Consult your local AECG before reaching out to any First Nations person or business
Lead with what you are offering — paid time, clear purpose, student outcomes
Allow a full school term for responses. Rushed requests signal disrespect
Pay for cultural knowledge. ICIP protocols require it. This is non-negotiable
Never list any First Nations person or business without their explicit written consent
Share back what students learned. This is a relationship, not a transaction

Where to begin

Start small.
Start on Gadigal Country.
Test it honestly.

Phase One is one or two Year 9 classes, one design brief cycle, two Gadigal markers, one term. It is not about getting it perfect. It is about building trust, learning what Country-centred design education actually looks like, and creating a model that First Nations communities want to be part of.
